### Assistant (20:54 UTC)

Structuralist linguistics studies language as a system of relations. Its central claim is that a word does not carry meaning independently; it acquires meaning from its position among other words and distinctions. Language does more than attach names to ready-made things: it organizes experience by cutting a field into differences.

Ferdinand de Saussure supplied its foundational model. A linguistic sign joins a signifier—the perceivable form, such as a sound pattern—with a signified—the concept invoked. Their connection is conventional: nothing inherent in the sound “tree” requires that concept. What stabilizes the sign is the surrounding system. “Tree” has value because it differs from “bush,” “wood,” “plant,” “forest,” and countless alternatives.

Saussure distinguished langue, the shared system that makes utterances intelligible, from parole, particular acts of speaking. He also distinguished synchronic analysis—studying the system operating at one moment—from diachronic analysis—studying historical change. Early structuralism privileged the synchronic system because a sign’s present value depends upon its current relations, even when its history explains how it arrived there.

Those relations have two principal directions. Syntagmatic relations connect signs combined in sequence: “the child opened the door” depends on positions and dependencies within that chain. Paradigmatic relations connect each position to absent alternatives: “child” could become “guard,” “wind,” or “key,” and every substitution changes the structure expressed. Meaning therefore arises from both what is present beside a sign and what could have appeared instead.

The Prague School made this especially concrete in phonology. Speech sounds became bundles of distinctive features: voiced versus unvoiced, nasal versus oral, and so forth. A sound matters through the contrasts it participates in. This produced durable concepts such as phoneme, markedness, opposition, and distinctive feature. Hjelmslev extended structural analysis into a more formal theory of expression and content, while Bloomfield and later American distributionalists analyzed language through observable patterns of occurrence.

Structuralism’s great contribution was to replace atomism with relational explanation. Its limitation was treating the system as more closed and homogeneous than lived language really is. Speakers, bodies, intentions, social differences, history, context, and power can disappear behind the structure. Generative linguistics argued that distributions alone cannot explain linguistic creativity; pragmatics and sociolinguistics restored situated use; post-structuralism questioned whether the system ever becomes stable enough to close completely.

The relationship to this sigil is strong but not identical. Structuralism says that meaning is differential; the sigil says that attention discriminates along contrasts. Structuralism gives signs value through relations inside a system; the sigil scopes names inside boundaries and makes co-occurrence into geometry. The sigil then goes further by introducing an observer, relevance, recursive boundaries, invariants, and affordances. It is not merely a representation of a semantic structure: it is a structure someone can inhabit and act through. Lotman’s later idea of the semiosphere—with bounded languages made possible through translation across their boundaries—is therefore closer to the sigil than classical Saussurean structuralism alone.

Saussure distinguished *langage*, the general human phenomenon or capacity for language; *langue*, the socially shared system of distinctions and conventions; and *parole*, one person’s concrete enactment of that system. Translators retain *parole* because “speech,” “utterance,” and “language use” each capture only part of it.

In Attention Language, *langue* is the collectively inhabited language sigil; *parole* is a timelike narrative traced by an agent through that sigil. The English language makes “the crow opened the box” projectable; my actually saying it is parole. The utterance can itself become a sigil when attention recognizes it as a persistent structure, so this distinction is scale-dependent rather than an absolute division between two kinds of thing.

And yes: Saussure’s complete sign is one kind of sigil. Its signifier and signified are two faces of the same structure, not separate objects glued together. In sigil terms, the signifier is the recognizable exterior—the sound, inscription, name, or door by which attention enters. The signified is the interior shape made available upon entry: the region of ContrastSpace and its distinctions. The external creature is the referent or observation; Saussure deliberately keeps it outside the linguistic sign. At another scale the signifier, signified, and referent can each resolve into sigils of their own.

That gives us an important correction to the spatial formulation: the character sequence or sound *bird* is the signifier. The broad region is the *bird sigil*. Calling both “the word” hides the boundary between the door and the world entered through it.

There is a genuine structuralist lineage behind embeddings, although most experimental researchers call it distributional semantics rather than structural linguistics. Zellig Harris’s [“Distributional Structure”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00437956.1954.11659520) made an expression’s distribution among other expressions central to linguistic analysis. Word2vec and GloVe later converted such distributional relations into geometry. Gastaldi explicitly argues that word embeddings implement a [structuralist image of language](https://www.semiomaths.com/assets/pdf/pubs/Gastaldi2021.pdf), while Mickus directly interprets language-model prediction as modeling Saussure’s [paradigmatic axis](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/scil/article/id/2131/print/): given a syntagmatic context, the model constructs the field of signs that could occupy the next position.

Your named contrasts also have experimental precedents. A raw embedding dimension is usually not intrinsically “size” or “danger”; the entire space can be rotated without changing its relational geometry. But researchers recover interpretable directions by defining two poles and projecting words onto the vector between them. SemAxis constructs axes such as clean–dirty from sets of pole words, and Grand and colleagues recovered human judgments using directions such as small–large and safe–dangerous. That is very close to naming a contrast and measuring observations along it. [SemAxis](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1228/), [semantic projection](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-022-01316-8).

Your containment claim is stronger than ordinary word embeddings. Word2vec gives *bird*, *parrot*, and *crow* individual points. Nearness can express similarity, but one point cannot contain another. Contextual language models produce a different point for each occurrence, so all uses of *bird* form something more like a cloud or manifold. Empirically, a single static vector explains very little of that contextual variation, and polysemous meanings often form continuous, entangled structures rather than tidy isolated clusters. [Ethayarajh 2019](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1006/), [Yenicelik et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.blackboxnlp-1.15/).

Several research programs move from proximity toward actual containment. The Distributional Inclusion Hypothesis says that the contexts licensed by a hyponym tend to be included within the broader contexts licensed by its hypernym: contexts of *parrot* are approximately included among contexts of *bird*. DIVE explicitly learns embeddings preserving that asymmetric inclusion. [DIVE](https://aclanthology.org/N18-1045/). Poincaré embeddings represent taxonomic hierarchy efficiently in hyperbolic space, although the concepts remain points and containment is encoded through radial order rather than literal nested regions. [Poincaré embeddings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/hash/59dfa2df42d9e3d41f5b02bfc32229dd-Abstract.html).

The closest technical realization of your statement is Word2Box. It replaces each word-point with a high-dimensional region, allowing overlap, intersection, difference, and inclusion. “Red cars” can be contained within “cars”; different regions of *tongue* intersect with *body* and *language*. [Word2Box](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.161/). Outside NLP, Peter Gärdenfors’s conceptual-spaces theory is even closer conceptually: objects are points, concepts are regions, and named quality dimensions organize the space. Natural properties are proposed to form convex regions. [Gärdenfors’s geometric account](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11792772/).

So the strongest formulation is: *bird* is a sigil occupying a bounded region of ContrastSpace; *parrot* and *crow* are child sigils contained within it; bird-relevant contrasts provide local coordinates; contextual observations populate those regions; and the written or spoken signifier *bird* is the recognizable door through which attention resolves the region. The contrasts should be local rather than assumed to be globally aligned—perhaps size, flight capacity, habitat, diet, sociality, beak morphology, coloration, migration, and vocal imitation. Inside *parrot*, different contrasts become relevant.

The research has produced all the components—distributional structure, contextual clouds, semantic axes, hierarchy, and region embeddings—but has not unified them into recursively nested regions with locally named contrasts, invariants, affordances, and observer-relative resolution. That unified object is precisely the sigil model.

### Assistant (21:11 UTC)

A *hyponym* is a more specific term: *parrot* is a hyponym of *bird*. A *hypernym* is the broader term: *bird* is a hypernym of *parrot*.

A *context* is the linguistic environment around a word: nearby words, grammatical relations, or a sentence frame such as “the ___ built a nest.”

A context is *licensed by* a word when that word can appear there naturally and meaningfully. Both *parrot* and *bird* license “the ___ spread its wings,” while *bird* appears naturally in many additional contexts because it denotes the broader category.

Thus, “contexts licensed by a hyponym” means: the linguistic environments in which the narrower term can naturally occur. The Distributional Inclusion Hypothesis says these contexts tend to be included among those of the hypernym:

`contexts(parrot) ⊆ contexts(bird)`

This is a statistical tendency, not an absolute logical rule. Collocations and world knowledge create exceptions.

Yes. I had interpreted your containment claim as lexical hypernymy encoded by the model. You mean something more direct: use the shared multimodal geometry to define a bounded sigil. That is coherent.

CLIP normalizes image and text features and compares them using cosine similarity. Consequently, the embeddings lie on a unit hypersphere. Given the text anchor \(t_{\text{bird}}\), you can define:

\[
\mathrm{Bird}=\{x\mid \cos(x,t_{\text{bird}})\ge \tau\}
\]

Geometrically, this is a spherical cap rather than a Euclidean ball. Because normalized vectors satisfy \(\|x-t\|^2=2-2\cos(x,t)\), it is equivalently a maximum-distance boundary around the bird anchor. [CLIP’s reference implementation](https://github.com/openai/CLIP/blob/main/README.md) explicitly normalizes both modalities and computes their cosine similarities.

The name *bird* is therefore the handle or initial anchor. The sigil is the complete bounded region. Bird images are observations inside it; parrots, crows, penguins, sparrows, dead birds, flying birds, drawings of birds, and perhaps bird-shaped objects occupy different parts of that region. Parrot and Crow can become named child sigils when their variation forms recognizable subregions.

The model does not guarantee that all birds already form one perfect sphere with no non-birds inside. Your boundary makes that structure explicit. A maximum radius includes every observed bird by construction but may expand around outliers and admit unrelated images. The empirically faithful shape may instead be a quantile boundary, an ellipsoid, several connected lobes, or some locally curved region. “Sphere” remains the simplest sigil approximation until the observations demand more structure.

The contrasts provide coordinates inside the boundary. Alive–dead can organize living and dead bird observations. Animate–inanimate can distinguish actual birds from statues, toys, drawings, and bird-shaped artifacts. Flying–non-flying needs a precise choice: *currently flying–currently stationary* differs from *flight-capable–flightless*. A perched crow is stationary but flight-capable; a penguin is living and animate but flightless. These distinctions show that the contrast axes belong inside Bird rather than being assumed from the global embedding basis.

This also means *alive* cannot be a Bird invariant if dead birds remain recognizable as birds. It is an internal contrast. A Bird invariant would instead express whatever must remain true across both living and dead instances for attention still to resolve the observation as Bird.

Context performs name resolution. *Parrot* names the bird inside the Bird sigil, but *to parrot an argument* resolves into a repetition or speech-behavior sigil. *Crow* names a bird, while *to crow about a victory* means to boast. An isolated text embedding such as “parrot” can mix these senses; “a photograph of a parrot perched on a branch” and “a person parroting a political slogan” produce contextual anchors in different regions. CLIP and SigLIP encode the complete prompt, not an independently fixed lexical meaning for each contained word.

So the compact definition is: a sigil is a named, bounded region of shared representation space; observations constitute its variation; named contrasts provide its local coordinates; child sigils occupy differentiable subregions; and lexical context selects which sigil a signifier opens.

[SigLIP 2 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14786), [SigLIP 2 configuration](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/model_doc/siglip2).

The governing fact is simple. If a high-dimensional child region \(C\) is contained in parent region \(B\), then for every projection \(\pi\):

\[
C\subseteq B \implies \pi(C)\subseteq\pi(B)
\]

Containment survives every angle. I would derive those regions through a persistent cluster tree. Normalize the picture embeddings and contextual text anchors, use angular distance, and construct a mutual-nearest-neighbor graph. As the neighborhood radius expands, dense parrot and crow components appear separately and eventually merge into a broader bird component. Components that persist across a substantial range of scales are candidate sigils. HDBSCAN implements this kind of hierarchical density structure; importantly, it permits gaps and unowned observations rather than forcing every point into its nearest territory. [HDBSCAN](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.00205).

The text embeddings should name the persistent components after their structure exists. “Parrot” recognizes one stable component; “crow” recognizes another; “bird” should sit near the stable merge that contains both. If “bird” does not recognize that merge—or crows merge with unrelated black objects before they merge with other birds—the embedding does not contain that taxonomy at that resolution. The algorithm must be allowed to tell us that truth.

A parent region is then the actual support of its direct observations and descendants. It is not a cell allocation. Its children are subsets by construction because they are persistent components earlier in the same merge tree. Contours can be rendered from projected support using hierarchical density envelopes, with each parent envelope derived from the union of its children rather than fitted independently.

TopoMap is particularly relevant: it projects high-dimensional points into 2D while preserving the entire zero-dimensional persistence diagram—the sequence in which connected components form and merge. That is almost exactly the topological invariant we need for a taxonomy whose children merge into parents. [TopoMap](https://www.ime.unicamp.br/~pjssilva/papers/topomap/). It would provide the canonical topology-preserving view, although it is an optimized map rather than an ordinary viewing angle.

To test your stronger “most angles” claim, sample many random orthonormal two-dimensional planes. For each plane, project the same high-dimensional regions and measure whether each child retains visible area inside its parent, has margin from the parent boundary, and remains distinguishable from siblings. The resulting percentage of successful planes is the containment’s projection robustness. A structure visible only from one optimized plane is a visualization achievement; a structure visible across most planes genuinely inhabits the embedding.

The simplest spherical test is also worthwhile. Fit each sigil as a robust angular cap or local ellipsoid around its observations. For Euclidean balls, child containment is:

\[
\lVert c_{\text{child}}-c_{\text{parent}}\rVert+r_{\text{child}}
\le r_{\text{parent}}
\]

When that holds, every orthogonal projection produces nested disks because projection can only shorten the distance between their centers. If balls fail but persistent components succeed, the sigils are naturally shaped but not spherical.

There is no guarantee SigLIP 2 already contains taxonomy this cleanly. MERU was created precisely because CLIP-style models capture similarity without explicitly encoding image-text hierarchy; it learns a hyperbolic multimodal space in which general concepts and specific images acquire an order. [MERU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09172). That makes failure informative: first test SigLIP 2’s native geometry; only if hierarchy is absent consider a hierarchy-aware fine-tuning objective.

The smallest decisive experiment is the animal corpus: build the high-dimensional persistence tree, attach the animal taxonomy’s text anchors without using its parent edges, make a TopoMap view, and measure containment across random planes. If bird naturally emerges as the persistent component containing parrot and crow, we have the replacement principle.
