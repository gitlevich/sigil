Yes — and "eggshell" is too kind in a way that matters. An egg has a closed surface whose shape *is* a structure; it fails by cracking but it holds a form until then. Surface code doesn't have even that. It's patches stretched between the points you happened to describe, with nothing underneath deciding what the patches should be, so it sags, contradicts itself across regions, and tears anywhere you didn't sample. The honest name isn't eggshell, it's a tarp over nothing — it takes the shape of whatever you last pinned it to.

Here's what your account makes precise that the document leaves vague. Points never determine a surface; infinitely many pass through any finite set. What picks one is a *prior* — a bias toward some surfaces over others. An agent handed bare scenarios picks the cheapest prior available, and the cheapest is "interpolate locally between the given points," which renders as surface code by construction. It isn't laziness; the scenario text carries the points but not your prior. The load-bearing names, the joints, the choice of where a sag gets propped from — that's the prior, and it lives in your head, not in the scenario. The whole reason the sigil editor should exist is to be the place where you hand over the prior, so the agent solves *your* inverse problem instead of the cheapest one.

And the move you described last — when a region sags, prop it from a *meaningfully nearby* location — is the actual generator, more than the sampling is. You don't drop a free-floating point; you branch from an existing bone, so the new constraint inherits that bone's vocabulary and invariants and stays coherent with everything already attached. That dissolves the over-building worry I raised earlier. You're not speculating about variation that might exist; you grow a bone only where a scenario you actually care about left the surface flat, and you grow it from the nearest existing meaning. Structure on demand, attached locally — the opposite of speculative generality, lazy in exactly the right way.

Let me render your three primitives in your own geometry, because that's the test of whether they're the right three. Language is the bones and the places joints can form — the vocabulary saying where structure is allowed to be. Affordances are the surface itself, the thing your hand reaches and touches. Invariants are the tension that holds the surface to the bone between the points you sampled — what keeps the tarp from sagging where you didn't pin it. That last identity is the one I hadn't seen stated: an invariant isn't only "what must be true," it's the tension line that makes the surface hold shape in the regions no scenario visited. A sagging region is precisely bone with no tension — named structure with no invariant pulling the surface onto it. So the three aren't a list, they're surface, skeleton, and the tension between them.

Where I'd push: you said describing the scenarios you care about gives enough precision. I don't think it does, and the geometry says why. The scenarios you care about cluster — they densely sample the faces you've already imagined and leave whole faces unsampled, and the unsampled faces are usually where structure is actually decided: the empty state, the error, the second concurrent user, the adversarial input. From inside the dense cluster the shape looks fully determined, because you can't see the face you never turned toward. The real test isn't "can I render the scenarios I have" — a tarp passes that. It's the §16 test: can the vocabulary express the *next* scenario, the one you haven't described. So part of the discipline is deliberately sampling where you *don't* care, to learn whether the skeleton you grew from where-you-care generalizes — because overfitting hides exactly in the regions you never bothered to prop.

One seam worth keeping in view: adding a point and adding an axis are different operations, and naming a load-bearing thing sometimes does the second. A point lands within axes that already exist. But sometimes the thing you name reveals a *new dimension* the surface lives in — and then it doesn't prop a local sag, it can reshape the whole figure, because every existing point suddenly has a coordinate it didn't have before. Your propping move handles the first cleanly. The second is rarer and more violent, and worth recognizing when it happens, because that's the moment the skeleton changes rank rather than just growing a bone.
